21 Months That Reshaped Hindi Cinema —

The Emergency Years at the Box Office.

Between the summer of 1975 and the spring of 1977, the Indian film industry worked under a regulatory regime that capped action sequences at 90 seconds, banned films mid-release, and forced one director to physically change his ending. The audience didn't stop coming. The films just changed.

For 21 months between the summer of 1975 and the spring of 1977, Hindi cinema worked under the tightest regulatory regime of its history. The censor board introduced new content rules. Films were submitted and held. Prints were physically destroyed. One filmmaker had to change his ending and would not be allowed to release his original cut for fifty years. The economic context was almost as difficult — the rupee weakened seventeen percent against the dollar in three years, the global financial architecture was being rewired around a new oil currency, and Indian cinema's largest overseas buyer was a state distributor in Moscow paying flat fees. What happened in those 21 months reshaped which films got made, who could afford to make them, and what the dominant Hindi film format would look like for the next decade.

Period definition
The Emergency was a 21-month period from 25 June 1975 to 21 March 1977, during which civil liberties were curtailed, the press was placed under pre-censorship, and a parallel set of regulatory directions were applied to the film industry through the Central Board of Film Certification (the censor board) and the Information & Broadcasting ministry. This article is about what those 21 months meant for cinema as a business. We have left the politics to historians who are better equipped for it.
I.Act One — The Regulation

The Rules Changed Inside the First Six Months.

The censor board's new guidance arrived in stages through the second half of 1975. The headline-level rules were specific and inflexible. Action sequences in any film were capped at six per release, with each individual sequence not exceeding 90 seconds of screen time. On-screen depictions of bloodshed were cut. Visible liquor bottles were cut. Several entire categories of content — political satire, references to the regulatory regime itself, anything that could be read as commentary on contemporary administration — were treated as automatic grounds for refusal of certification.

The film industry adapted in real time. Post-production work in Bombay's recording studios shifted heavily toward post-synced dialogue, replacing on-location sound. Foley artists worked overtime substituting exaggerated sound effects for action footage that had been clipped or compressed. Editors learned to assemble action sequences within the new time limits — six punches in 90 seconds rather than the longer choreographed set-pieces that had defined the early-decade dacoit films and Western imitations. The most cited contemporary observation about this period, from the industry memoir Words Sounds Images: A History of Media and Entertainment in India, is also the most telling: even spots of blood were cut out.

Sholay's ending — censored, then restored fifty years later

The most famous casualty of the new regulations released into the period itself. Sholay was theatrically released on 15 August 1975, just fifty-one days after the Emergency was declared. The film had been completed earlier and submitted for certification before the new content rules were finalised; the censor board's response was to require changes to the ending. In the original cut, the character Thakur Baldev Singh — a former police officer whose family has been killed by the dacoit Gabbar Singh — kills Gabbar in the climactic confrontation. The censor board declined to certify this ending. A former police officer killing a dacoit outside the legal process, the board ruled, was incompatible with the contemporary emphasis on law-and-order discipline. The makers shot an alternative ending in which the police arrive in time, Thakur hesitates, and Gabbar is taken into custody. That is the ending that ran for five and a half years at Bombay's Minerva Theatre and that became part of the popular culture of the film.

The original cut wasn't seen in cinemas until 12 December 2025, when "Sholay: The Final Cut" — a 4K restoration completed by the Film Heritage Foundation with full Dolby 5.1 audio — released worldwide. The reinstated ending and the longer 204-minute cut waited fifty years and four months for a theatrical audience.

Aandhi, and the politics of fictional resemblance

Aandhi released in February 1975, four months before the regulatory period began. It told the story of a woman politician whose marriage had strained under the demands of her career. Sanjeev Kumar and Suchitra Sen played the leads. Audiences who watched the film in its first few weeks read the protagonist as a fictional figure with surface resemblances to a real, contemporary public figure. Once the new regulatory regime was in place, that resemblance became sufficient reason to suspend the film's certification. Aandhi was pulled from theatres and held off-circulation for the duration of the period. It premiered on the state broadcaster in 1977 after the elections that ended the Emergency, with the certification restored. The film's underground reputation through those two years did more for its eventual cultural status than its initial theatrical run had managed.

Kissa Kursi Ka — submitted in April 1975, burned at a factory

Kissa Kursi Ka is the period's most physically extraordinary cinema story. The film was a political satire — broadly directed at the same regime that would, two months after its submission to the censor board in April 1975, declare the Emergency. The board issued a show-cause notice raising over fifty objections. By the time the producer's reply was filed in July, the regulatory regime was already in effect.

What happened next is documented in the report of an inquiry commission established after the period ended. All available prints of Kissa Kursi Ka — the master print held at the censor board's Bombay office, plus every distribution copy — were physically moved to an industrial facility outside Delhi and burned. The film was, in effect, deleted from the historical record. The 1977 inquiry commission named specific individuals as responsible and recommended action; that action was taken through the criminal courts later in the decade. A remake of Kissa Kursi Ka with a new cast was produced and released in 1977 after the period ended. The original remains the only Hindi film whose negative was destroyed by directive.

These three cases — Sholay's altered ending, Aandhi's suspended certification, Kissa Kursi Ka's destruction — are not the full extent of the period's censorship history. They are the cases that left clearest documentary records. Many more films had cuts demanded, scenes deleted, dialogue softened, and submissions delayed in ways that were noted in the industry trade press at the time but never formally compiled.

II.Act Two — The Films

The Releases That Carried 1975, 1976, and Early 1977.

Despite the regulatory friction, the period produced a release calendar of remarkable depth. The 1975 calendar was loaded — Sholay headlined a year that also included Deewaar, Aandhi, Mausam, Jai Santoshi Maa, Julie, Chupke Chupke, and Khel Khel Mein. Several of these were already in late production or post-production when the regulatory regime began, which meant they reached theatres without major content modification. The films greenlit AFTER June 1975, however, looked structurally different — and the difference became visible across the 1976 release calendar.

Sholay's ₹35 crore lifetime is reported in 1975 nominal rupees from contemporary trade compilations. The figure has been re-quoted hundreds of times since; some recent inflation-adjusted estimates put the equivalent value above ₹3,000 crore in 2025 terms. Jai Santoshi Maa, the surprise devotional hit, did its business overwhelmingly through women's matinee audiences in B and C-circuit cinemas — a footfall that the trade press of the time was structurally unprepared to track.

The pattern that defined the year is visible in the top five. Three of them — Nagin, Hera Pheri, Kabhi Kabhie — are multi-starrer ensembles. Nagin carried eight named leads in a single horror-fantasy that gave Reena Roy her star-making role. Hera Pheri put Amitabh Bachchan and Vinod Khanna together for the first time, supported by Saira Banu and Sulakshana Pandit. Kabhi Kabhie ran with six leads across two generations — Amitabh, Shashi Kapoor, Rakhee, Waheeda Rehman, Rishi Kapoor, Neetu Singh.

The multi-starrer wasn't invented in 1976. Nasir Husain's Yaadon Ki Baaraat (1973) is generally credited as the modern template, and Sholay (1975) executed it at the highest level. But 1976 was the year the format became the default. Three of the top five Hindi grossers that year were ensembles with five or more named leads. By 1977, four of the top five would be.

The first three months of 1977 fell within the regulatory period. The remaining nine months — including the May release of Amar Akbar Anthony — fell outside. But the production decisions had been made in 1976. The films released in 1977 had been written, cast, and financed during the regulated months, and they reflect that period's commercial logic. The censorship loosened. The formula did not.

III.Act Three — The Banners

Who Was Making These Films, and How They Were Structured.

Hindi cinema in 1975–77 was a family-owned cottage industry. Production companies were almost universally privately held, frequently multi-generational, and run by individuals who often produced AND directed AND distributed AND financed the same project. The corporate studio model that defined Hollywood — and that would eventually arrive in India in the 2000s — did not exist. The dominant structure was the producer-director's personal banner, backed by syndicated financing from trading families and exhibition partners.

Tier 1 · The studios that defined the era
Sippy Films
G.P. Sippy (founder) · Ramesh Sippy (director, Sholay) · Sindhi exhibition family
The defining production of the period came from a banner that had been distributing films since the 1950s before turning producer. G.P. Sippy spent ₹3 crore on Sholay — the highest budget in Hindi cinema's history at that point — to make a 198-minute multi-genre experiment that early critics declared a flop. The film recovered through word-of-mouth, ran 286 weeks at Bombay's Minerva Theatre, and became the studio's permanent identity. Sippy Films would never again produce anything at this scale.
Rajshri Productions
Founded 1947 · Tarachand Barjatya · Distribution-plus-production combine
The oldest of the surviving major banners and the most consistent through the period. Released Chitchor (Jan 30, 1976) and the slate of family-oriented films that the studio became known for. Rajshri may be the only major banner that worked through the full 21 months entirely unaffected by content censorship — their films inherently avoided everything the censor board was watching for. The studio's late-70s "middle cinema" run made Ravindra Jain a household-name composer and would set the template for the family-musical formula the studio rode all the way to Maine Pyar Kiya (1989) and Hum Aapke Hain Koun (1994).
Yash Raj Films
Founded 1970 · Yash Chopra · Romance-drama prestige banner
Released Kabhi Kabhie (Feb 27, 1976) during the period — the studio's first ensemble showcase and the film that established YRF's romance-poetry identity. Chopra had directed Deewaar (1975) for Trimurti Films before YRF fully consolidated as a prestige banner. By 1976, the studio was small but ambitious. Trishul (1978) and Silsila (1981) were still ahead.
Tier 2 · The producer-directors with personal banners
Nasir Husain Films
Founded by Nasir Husain · Multi-starrer template architect
Husain had launched the modern multi-starrer template with Yaadon Ki Baaraat (1973), written by the Salim-Javed duo. Through the Emergency, his studio developed what became Hum Kisise Kum Naheen (released Aug 25, 1977 — five months after the period ended). The studio's significance is structural rather than commercial: this is where the formula that defined the late-70s and 80s was first stitched together.
Prakash Mehra Productions
Founded by Prakash Mehra · Action-comedy specialist
After Zanjeer (1973) gave Mehra his first big Amitabh collaboration, he released Hera Pheri (Oct 1, 1976) during the period — the Amitabh and Vinod Khanna pairing that the studio would extend through Muqaddar Ka Sikandar (1978), Laawaris (1981), and the entire angry-young-man arc of the late 70s.
N.C. Sippy / Hrishikesh Mukherjee productions
N.C. Sippy (producer, no relation to G.P. Sippy of Sholay)
The "middle cinema" banner. Released Chupke Chupke (1975), Arjun Pandit (Apr 16, 1976), and Mili (1975). Mukherjee's films traffic in dialogue, character, and domestic comedy — not action, politics, or anything else the new content rules were targeting. They survived the period by being structurally invisible to the regulation, and arguably benefited from operating in a category that the formula banners had vacated.
Shankar Movies
Founded by Rajkumar Kohli · Producer-director both
Released Nagin (Jan 19, 1976) — the highest-grossing Hindi film of 1976. Kohli is the clearest example of the period's auteur-producer model: he financed his own films, directed them, and assembled multi-starrer ensembles. Nagin's eight-lead cast was the template he would extend to Jaani Dushman (1979), another nine-lead ensemble that defined the formula's late-decade peak.
B.R. Films
Founded 1950s · B.R. Chopra (Yash Chopra's older brother)
Released Chhoti Si Baat (Jan 9, 1976), directed by Basu Chatterjee — a romance-comedy with a cult afterlife. The B.R. Films output through the late 70s focused on social-message films and family dramas. The Chopra family operated two distinct prestige banners — B.R. Films and YRF — through the period, with B.R. Films the older and more socially-oriented of the two.
Tier 3 · Founded during or immediately after
Dharma Productions
Founded 1976 — during the period · Yash Johar
Founded inside the regulated months. No releases in the 1975–77 window — Dharma's first film, Dostana, didn't arrive until 1980. Worth noting mainly as evidence that new capital was entering film production even during the toughest regulatory environment. Johar was a former Sippy Films executive who used his exit to launch independently.
Eros International
Founded 1977 — immediately after the period ended · Arjun Lulla
Launched in 1977 as a distribution-focused company. Would become a major distribution power in the 80s and one of the first Indian companies to systematically pursue overseas markets — the Gulf and the UK in particular.
What the production house map reveals
The dominant Hindi cinema banner of 1976 was a family-owned single-film studio. The structural pattern across the top of the box office was producer-director identity tied to capital — the same individual conceived the film, directed it, raised the money for it, and often controlled its distribution. Amar Akbar Anthony's producer credit (1977) names three separate entities — Manmohan Films, MKD Films Combine, and Hirawat Jain & Company — for what was essentially one film by one director. Financing was syndicated, not studio-bankrolled. New banners entered the industry during the regulated period (Dharma in 1976) and immediately after (Eros in 1977). Capital kept flowing despite the regulatory headwind. That detail tells you more about the period than any single film's box office number.

IV.Act Four — The Money

What ₹8.5 Crore Actually Meant in 1976.

The economic context of the period requires three pieces of information: what a cinema ticket cost, what the rupee was worth in dollars, and what the industry's annual output added up to. Together, those three numbers explain why an ₹8.5 crore lifetime grosser was a blockbuster and why a ₹3 crore film budget was the biggest investment Hindi cinema had ever made.

Average ticket price: ₹2.00

Bombay's Minerva Theatre, where Sholay ran continuously from August 1975 onwards, charged between ₹1.50 and ₹3.00 for a ticket depending on seat class. The lower stall and front rows were ₹1.50 to ₹2.00. The middle rows were ₹2.50. The balcony, the most expensive seat in the house, was ₹3.00. This is documented through a preserved ticket stub from a 1975 Sholay screening that has been verified by multiple sources, including the actor Amitabh Bachchan, who shared a higher-priced "special show" stub from Old Delhi's Excelsior Theatre that ran at ₹20 — a premium showing, not the regular price.

For the article's working number, the period's average ticket price across all theatre tiers and city categories was approximately ₹2.00. The footfall math that follows uses this figure as the baseline.

At ₹2 a ticket, Nagin's lifetime nett of ₹8.5 crore translates to approximately 4.25 crore footfalls in India. Sholay's ₹35 crore lifetime translates to 17.5 crore footfalls, which aligns reasonably with the lower-end estimates of the film's total ticket sales. These are numbers that 2026's biggest Hindi films do not approach. Dhurandhar 2 sold roughly 3.2 crore tickets in India in 2026 against a ₹1,024 crore lifetime nett — a fraction of Sholay's footfall at a vastly higher ticket price.

The rupee depreciated about 17% against the dollar from 1973 to 1976. In dollar terms, Sholay's ₹3 crore budget was approximately $360,000 in 1975 USD. The entire top-of-call-sheet cast — Dharmendra (₹1.5 lakh), Sanjeev Kumar (₹1.25 lakh), Amitabh Bachchan (₹1 lakh), Hema Malini (₹75,000), Amjad Khan (₹50,000), Salim-Javed signing (₹10,000) — added up to under ₹5 lakh, or roughly $60,000 in 1975 dollars. The biggest Hindi film of the decade was made for the price of a small Hollywood independent.

The petrodollar question

The global currency architecture was being rewired around Indian cinema's release calendar, though the industry didn't yet know what to do with the information. The Bretton Woods system — under which the dollar had been pegged to gold and other currencies pegged to the dollar — had collapsed in August 1971. The Arab oil embargo arrived in October 1973. And the formal petrodollar system — the agreement under which Saudi Arabia would accept only US dollars for oil in exchange for American security commitments — was signed on 8 June 1974, exactly one year and seventeen days before the Indian regulatory period began.

That one-year gap matters. The petrodollar created a vast new flow of US dollars into the Gulf states, which financed a construction boom across the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman. That construction boom needed labour. The first significant wave of Indian migration to the Gulf — primarily from Kerala and Punjab — began in 1974 and accelerated through the late 1970s. By 1983, the Indian population in the Gulf states had reached approximately 1.2 million, up from about 40,000 in 1971. Hindi cinema followed the workers, but the Gulf as a Hindi film market would not mature until the 1980s. Through the period itself, the Gulf was a minor overseas territory.

V.Act Five — The Overseas

The Largest Overseas Market for Indian Films Was the Soviet Union.

Through the Emergency period and for fourteen more years afterwards — until the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991 — the largest overseas buyer of Indian films was a single state distributor. Sovexportfilm, the Soviet Ministry of Culture's foreign-acquisition arm, acquired Hindi films through flat-fee licensing deals. The producer received a predictable sum in dollars or roubles. The Soviet state received the right to dub the film into multiple languages and distribute it across all fifteen Soviet republics, often with print runs in the hundreds.

The Soviet Foundation · 1951
Awaara — the film that built the audience.
Raj Kapoor's Awaara released in the Soviet Union in 1954 with 800 prints across all fifteen republics. It is generally credited with around 100 million Soviet ticket sales across its theatrical lifetime. The title song "Awaara Hoon" became one of the most recognised pieces of foreign music in Russia and China for generations. Every Indian film that found Soviet distribution through the Emergency era and beyond was building on the audience Awaara had created.

Poster via TMDB.

The numerical scale of the Soviet audience for Indian cinema is consistently understated in the modern Hindi film conversation, which has organised itself around the diaspora-first overseas model of the 1990s onwards. In the 1970s, those diaspora markets did not yet exist at the scale required to support theatrical releases. The Soviet Union absorbed the gap.

Sholay's Soviet release didn't happen until May 1979, four years after its India release and two years after the regulatory period ended. The 60 million USSR ticket sales it generated represented a financial flow that — at the typical flat-fee deal structure of the era — likely netted the producers between ₹50 lakh and ₹1 crore. Significant, but not lifetime-defining. The deeper value of the Soviet market was its consistency. Films that flopped in India could still recover overseas costs through a Sovexportfilm deal. The Soviet market was the safety net for the entire industry.

East Africa and the Uganda inflection

Through the 1960s and into the early 1970s, East Africa — particularly Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Zambia — accounted for between 20% and 50% of overseas revenues for many Indian films. The South Asian population in East Africa had built urban cinema circuits dating to the colonial period; Hindi films, along with Egyptian Arabic films, were more popular than Hollywood in these markets through the 1960s.

That market structure was disrupted in 1972, when Uganda's government ordered the expulsion of approximately 80,000 South Asian residents — most of whom had lived in the country for two or more generations. The expulsion reshaped the regional cinema economy. The expelled families resettled primarily in the United Kingdom, with smaller communities moving to Canada and India. In the UK, the resettled population — concentrated in London, Leicester, and Birmingham — became the foundation of the first significant British market for Hindi cinema. The Uganda expulsion is generally understood as a political and humanitarian event. It was also, incidentally, the moment the UK's Hindi cinema audience was effectively born.

The Period in Summary
21 Months. Three Currencies. A New Formula.
Between June 1975 and March 1977, the Indian film industry navigated content regulation at home, a depreciating rupee, an emerging petrodollar that would eventually create the Gulf migration wave, and a Soviet state distributor that was the single largest buyer of its output. The multi-starrer format that defined the next decade of Hindi cinema was already in motion before the period began. The regulation accelerated it. Risk-spreading across an ensemble cast was a hedge against any single performer's project being held up in certification. Family-owned banners syndicated financing across multiple trading houses to absorb the friction. Middle-cinema directors found a competitive opening because their films were structurally invisible to the new content rules. By the spring of 1977 the regulatory regime ended. The economic structures it had shaped — the multi-starrer, the syndicated producer model, the Soviet-dependent overseas account — lasted into the late 1980s. The films looked different on the screen. The industry that made them had been reshaped.
