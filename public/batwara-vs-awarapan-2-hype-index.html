Boxoffy Hype Index · The Clash · Independence Day, Aug 14 2026

Nineteen years later, Sunny and Emraan clash again.

On 29 June 2007, Sunny Deol's Apne opened against Emraan Hashmi's Awarapan and won roughly six to one. On 14 August 2026, the eve of Independence Day, Batwara 1947 meets Awarapan 2 — two very different films, one holiday weekend, and a fight that's really about screens, not tickets.

Sunny won that Friday almost six to one. But the part that reframes the rematch is this: Awarapan lost money in cinemas and then became a cult record on the strength of its soundtrack, played and re-played for nineteen years until the sequel could exist at all. So the 2007 result is both a warning and the reason Awarapan 2 is even here. One film won the weekend; the other won the decade after it. Both men know exactly which lane they're in this time.

The two run-rates point in opposite directions, and that's the whole contest. Sunny Deol opens big when the subject is right — Gadar 2 gave him the biggest day-one of his life at ₹40.10 Cr, and even the lesser Jaat cracked a strong opening before its legs gave out. He is a front-loaded, North-belt, event-day star, and Independence Day with a partition film is about as "right subject" as it gets. Emraan Hashmi has never opened a film on his name — his hits are slow burns that music and word-of-mouth carry into profit. Awarapan itself is the proof: a flop that outlived most of its year's hits. So Batwara is built to win the opening, and Awarapan 2 is built to survive it. The question each has to answer is different.

The teaser genuinely trended at No. 1 on nostalgia — "goosebumps," "the OG is back." Then Ve Junoon arrived and the comments cooled: compared unfavourably to the original's music, some saying it "sounds like Saiyaara," questions about Disha Patani's fit against Shriya Saran's original. For a franchise the first film built on its soundtrack, a lukewarm first song is the exact wrong signal.

The sentiment gap mirrors the run-rates. Batwara's buzz is broad and steady — the profile of a film that opens. Awarapan 2's is spiky: a huge nostalgic teaser, then a music stumble on the one axis the franchise can't afford to stumble on. Nostalgia gets people to the teaser; it doesn't get them to the ticket window unless the songs land, and that's still an open question three weeks out.

Batwara will build its Day 1 from advance plus heavy same-day walk-ins in the North and single-screen belt — the Gadar audience that turns up for Sunny on a patriotic occasion without booking a week ahead. Awarapan 2 will barely register in advance and has to earn its number from multiplex word-of-mouth once the film is out. That's why their real contest isn't head-to-head presales. It's the exhibitor's whiteboard on Thursday night: on a two-Hindi-film holiday frame, whoever wins the screen and showtime allocation has half the battle won before a ticket is sold — and a partition film on Independence Day is the easier "yes" for a theatre owner.

Batwara opens roughly two-and-a-half to three times Awarapan on Day 1, and the Independence Day Saturday widens that gap before word-of-mouth even starts. But the day-one number isn't the verdict for either. Batwara is a ₹150-Cr-plus budget carrying Santoshi, Rahman and a big cast — it needs to hold through the long weekend and beyond, not just open. Awarapan 2 is a far smaller film that only has to clear a modest number to be a hit, so its low opening is less damning than it looks — if the music finally catches and the multiplex crowd shows, it wins its own, smaller race. The clean read: Batwara wins the weekend, Awarapan 2 plays for the month.

What each film should do to build hype.

Three weeks out, both campaigns have specific, fixable gaps. Here's the Boxoffy recommendation for each.

Batwara 1947 — convert respect into urgency
  • Drop the Rahman song that carries the emotion, now. The teasers sold the dialogue and the scale; the one weapon still holstered is the music. A big Rahman–Javed Akhtar track is what turns "I respect this" into "I'm booking Friday."
  • Separate itself from Gadar 2 explicitly. The "another partition film" comment is the only real drag. Lean the trailer into what's different — Santoshi's craft, the play it's based on, the humanity angle — rather than the war spectacle that invites the comparison.
  • Sell the Independence Day occasion. Position Friday-Saturday as the patriotic watch of the year. Single-screen block-booking and morning shows on the 15th are free money if the campaign frames it as an event, not just a release.
  • Use Aamir Khan's name louder. He's the producer and a huge draw; his visible backing reassures a mass audience that this is a quality event film, not a routine period drama.
Awarapan 2 — fix the music, then the presence
  • The next song has to land, and it has to sound like Awarapan. Ve Junoon underwhelmed on the one axis this franchise lives or dies on. The second single is close to a must-win — bring back the brooding Mithoon-Emraan sound the original is loved for, not a trend-chasing track.
  • Address the Disha-vs-Shriya doubt head-on. The comparison is already loud. A strong character-reveal for Disha Patani — acting, not glamour — defuses the biggest recurring criticism before release.
  • Trade day-one for word-of-mouth. Emraan won't out-open Sunny, so stop trying. Screen premieres and early word for the multiplex crowd, targeting a Saturday jump, is the realistic path — the film's whole model is the hold.
  • Own the counter-programming lane. Be loudly the other option — the romance for audiences who don't want a war film on the holiday. Two different genres is an advantage if Awarapan 2 claims its half of the room instead of fighting for Batwara's.
