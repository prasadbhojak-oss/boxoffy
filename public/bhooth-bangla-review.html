Bhooth Bangla Runs on Nostalgia
and Earns Its Horror Backstory.
This Has Legs.

Boxoffy Team · Apr 19, 2026 · 5 min read · Watched in a packed house, Saturday evening
3.5
out of 5
ENTERTAINING · HAS LEGS

Let's start with the audience, because that's the real review. Saturday evening, packed house, multiple generations — grandparents, parents, teenagers, the works. For long stretches, especially in that first hour, this crowd was in splits. Not the polite laughter of people who feel obligated. The kind that starts somewhere in your chest and gets away from you. That's a data point no critic aggregate captures. And it matters enormously for understanding what Bhooth Bangla is and isn't trying to be.

The First Half: This Is Why Priyadarshan Exists

The first thing that announces itself isn't a joke or a ghost — it's the mansion. Long corridors, light falling in the wrong places, a silence that feels like it has weight. Priyadarshan, for all his reputation as a comedy director, has always understood setting. He builds the world before he starts pulling the rug out from under you. And then the chaos arrives, and it arrives correctly.

Akshay Kumar, Asrani ji, and Rajpal Yadav together in a frame are a force of nature. All three have gotten older, yes. But the timing? The physical comedy? The specific frequency on which they operate when Priyadarshan is directing — all still there. The Akshay-Rajpal scenes are the high point of the first half. The tu-tu main-main energy, misunderstandings escalating beautifully into full absurdity — this is Priyadarshan doing what only he knows how to do. Nobody else in Hindi cinema directs ensemble comedy with this kind of controlled pandemonium.

The Nokia 1100 torch moment is the scene of the film. Akshay encountering the ghost, armed with nothing but a feature phone's flashlight — it lands the joke, sets the timeline, earns the biggest laugh of the night. One image tells you exactly which era this story lives in, without a word of exposition. That's the Priyadarshan who made us love these films.

The Nokia 1100 is also clever world-building. It reminded us of that classic Nokia commercial — the indestructible phone with the torch that became cultural shorthand for a certain kind of Indian 2000s life. The film is soaked in that era's texture, and for audiences who lived it, that nostalgia does serious heavy lifting — sometimes more than the script itself.

The Screenplay: Where It Gets Honest

Here is the thing about the Priyadarshan of Hera Pheri and Hungama that people forget when they talk about those films. The comedy in the classics — written largely by the late Neeraj Vora — emerged from the situation. Babu Bhaiya, Shyam and Raju didn't do funny things. They were caught in funny situations that their personalities made funnier. The absurdity was a consequence of character. There's a difference, and it's everything in this genre.

In Bhooth Bangla, the comedy is often imposed on the situation rather than growing out of it. The screenplay — written by Priyadarshan, Abilash Nair and Rohan Shankar — has real strengths. Rohan Shankar's dialogue in particular is sharp in the funny moments, and a handful of one-liners bring the house down. The original story by Akash Kaushik has genuine potential. But the larger construction is looser than it needed to be, and it shows.

The Screenplay's Central Structural Problem

The entire premise — Arjun bringing his sister's wedding to the haunted mansion at Mangalpur — only works if you accept one foundational convenience: his father, Vasudev Acharya, cannot be reached. Vasudev knows the full backstory of Vadhusur. He knows exactly why no wedding should happen in that village. If Meera had simply been able to call her father before Arjun finalised the venue, the story collapses before it even begins.

The writers knew this. Their solution is to render Vasudev unreachable for the entire first act. It's the kind of plot scaffolding that holds the house up but becomes visible the moment you look for it. And the film takes nearly an hour and a half to complete what should be twenty minutes of setup — largely because of how hard it's working to justify this convenience.

The repetition problem compounds this. Priyadarshan's comedy has always worked through escalation — a running gag that gets worse and funnier with each iteration, but always introduces a new wrinkle. Here, some gags simply repeat without variation. Paresh Rawal's backside catching fire once is funny; three times at the same beat starts to feel like the screenplay is covering for a lack of new ideas in that stretch. The editing could have solved some of this — most of what the film is criticised for being too long about is fixable with a tighter cut, perhaps fifteen minutes shorter in the first half.

None of this kills the film. The audience in our house was laughing through most of it regardless. But the distance between what Bhooth Bangla is and what it could have been with a tighter screenplay is real, and it's worth naming honestly.

The Performances: An Honest Card

This film rests almost entirely on Akshay, Asrani ji and Rajpal Yadav — and they hold it up. All three are in real form. Akshay is in the genre he was born for. His comic timing in the ensemble sequences is as sharp as it's been in years. Asrani ji — in what may be among his last screen appearances — is quietly magnificent. His scenes carry a warmth that goes beyond the comedy; there is genuine tenderness in the way Priyadarshan uses him. Rajpal Yadav dominates every sequence he's in.

Paresh Rawal is below his usual energy — something feels slightly muted, which is unusual for him in Priyadarshan material. The three-way dynamic doesn't reach maximum voltage as a result. Mithila Palkar is underrated in the ensemble; she has the most grounded role and handles it dependably. Wamiqa Gabbi is pretty and present in a significant subplot, though her track doesn't fully earn its narrative weight.

Tabu is the most significant misfire — and none of this is about her ability. The role demands physical authority: the capacity to dance with genuine conviction, to carry a flashback with the weight of mythology behind it. She cannot dance, and the film needs her to. Those sequences expose the mismatch badly. Madhuri Dixit would have absolutely eaten this role. The grace, the classical movement, the emotional gravity — this character was written for someone in that register, and the film pays a real price for the miscasting.

The Second Half: Vadhusur, and the Payoff

The second half pivots hard from comedy to mythology. Priyadarshan dials the laughs down and leans into the Vadhusur origin — how he came to possess the village, what the curse means, and the rules around how he can be contained. This is the film's most carefully written material. The backstory is well-researched; you feel the mythology rather than just being told it.

The crux of the film rests on a specific detail — what device is used to keep Vadhusur at bay, and more importantly, how it's deployed. That reveal is the best moment in the second half. We won't say more here. Go in knowing as little as possible.

The finale is a proper action block and it lands. The VFX is competent without being in your face — it serves the story rather than overwhelming it. The violence reads as mythological-grade, in the register of how Ramayana and Mahabharata adaptations depict combat. This is squarely a family film — the horror is atmospheric, not gory.

One screenplay problem lingers. The grandfather's opposition to Arjun in the climax feels inadequately explained — as if the scene that was supposed to connect their conflict was left on the editing table. The audience has been led to believe they want the same thing. The reason they end up on opposite sides in the finale isn't satisfyingly resolved. It's possible the explanation existed and was cut. If so, that was a mistake.

The Music: The One That Got Away

Pritam is one of Hindi cinema's finest composers, which is what makes Bhooth Bangla his most forgettable output in years. This is a Priyadarshan film — it needed at least one song that becomes part of the cultural memory of the film. An Ami Je Tomar moment. Something you hum on the way out and still associate with the movie three years later. That song never arrives. The tracks are pleasant, but they pop up at the wrong moments and interrupt the film's rhythm rather than elevating it. One genuinely great anthem would have changed the word-of-mouth conversation significantly. Its absence is the film's quietest missed opportunity.

So Should You Watch It?

Yes. Go with your family. Go for the first half, stay for the Vadhusur origin. Lower your expectations about screenplay tidiness, raise them for ensemble comedy. Accept this for what it is — a film that lives on atmosphere and performance, not plotting.

The packed house we were in laughed through most of it. The family crowd will keep coming. The nostalgia is real and earns its keep. Akshay Kumar in the genre he was built for, with Asrani ji and Rajpal Yadav beside him, is worth the ticket price by itself.

Our BCM model has Bhooth Bangla at ₹118 Cr base-case lifetime. After watching it in a full house on Saturday, we are not inclined to revise that downward. If anything, the family audience signal was stronger than expected. This film will show longer legs for Akshay and Priyadarshan — and deliver them another hit.

3.5 out of 5. And if you figure out the full logic of what happens with the grandfather in the climax, we'd genuinely like to know. That's the Reddit thread this film deserves.
